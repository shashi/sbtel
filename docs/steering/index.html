Steering with sbtel

Your agent can write faster than you can read. What it cannot do is decide what should be built. That part is yours, and it runs on the one thing that does not scale: your attention. This guide shows how to steer the work from one diagram, however many agents are doing it, and why sbtel is built to spend your attention with care.

#How sbtel helps focus

With coding agents, doing the work is rarely the hard part. Keeping track is: what you asked for, what came back, what is still open, and which part of the system each of those belongs to. In a chat all of it is one stream, and the structure lives in your head.

sbtel puts the structure on a diagram that you and your agent share: the parts of the system, the levels they sit on, and what passes between them. Everything said about a part is said on that part, and stays there.

This is not a console for running many agents. sbtel separates two things that usually come tied together: how many agents are working, and how many things you are thinking about. You talk to one agent, through the map. It may do all the work itself or hand parts of it to others; that is its business, and you never need to know. What you get either way is the whole context in one place, and a lane for each thing: a thread on the block it concerns. A question about Sync does not get mixed into the conversation about Store. Lanes still mean moving between them, so you choose how many there are: close a block, and everything said on the parts inside it reads as one thread, in one place.

The diagram is doing real work. Jill Larkin and Herbert Simon showed why a diagram can beat the same facts written as sentences: it puts together in space what belongs together, so you find things by looking, not by holding them in your head. A chat scrolls away. A map stays where you left it.

#How to steer

Six habits. Each takes a minute to learn, and together they are the whole skill.

#Say it where it belongs

Select a block, press C and write. The comment lives on that block, so you never have to explain where you mean, and your agent reads it with the block it is about.

#Let replies wait

Nothing in sbtel announces itself. When your agent answers, a dot appears on the block and on the reply button, and that is all: no sound, no banner, nothing sliding in. You look when you reach a stopping point of your own.

#Walk the replies

Press ]. sbtel takes you to the nearest new reply and opens it. Press again for the next one. It finishes the neighbourhood you are in before it moves you anywhere else, and when nothing is left the button goes dim. [ takes you back.

#Read a nod as a nod

When your agent has nothing to ask, it answers with a nod: a 👍 on your comment. It shows in the thread and lights no dot, so “got it” never costs you a visit. A dot always means there are words to read.

#Zoom out

Press Space on a block you are not working on, and it closes. Everything said inside it becomes one thread on the closed block: one dot, one stop on the walk. Open it again when that part is your work.

#Let the layout set the order

The walk starts with what is nearest to you. If you would sooner have it start over each time, open the menu on the reply count and choose from the beginning. Then the diagram is your priority list: put a block earlier and its replies reach you first.

#Why it is built this way

Every habit above protects the same thing. Gloria Mark and her colleagues interrupted people in the middle of their work and found that they finished just as fast, and paid for it in stress, frustration and effort. So sbtel never interrupts: a reply is a dot, and a dot can wait. Sophie Leroy found that when you leave one task for another, part of your attention stays behind, and the new task gets what is left. So the walk finishes one neighbourhood before it moves you, and a file, a picture or a calendar invite that your agent points to opens on the map, not in another window. E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that an unfinished goal keeps intruding on your thoughts until it has a plan. So every open question has a place, in the lane of its own block, and the queue has an end you can reach. Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown asked for technology that “will move easily from the periphery of our attention, to the center, and back”. That is what a dot is. And what sbtel leaves out is everything that would steer for you: your pace, your priorities and your breaks stay your own.

#Try it

Say this to your coding agent:

Hey Claude, could you install sbtel (shashi.biz/sbtel/llm.txt) for me and show me the plan in it?

When the diagram opens, select a block, press C and say what you want. Using sbtel covers installing by hand and everything on the canvas, and the playground lets you try the canvas with no agent at all.
