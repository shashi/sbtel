Why

sbtel gives you an opinionated, WYSIWYG view of any system at any level of granularity, so you can collaborate with AI on design, understanding, and architectural bug fixes. It comes with a linter the AI can use to make sure the code matches the picture.

Design usually starts on a whiteboard. It’s our room to think. You can take a picture of it and show it to your agent, but after eight hours of feature engineering in a chat, it’s hard to feel you still know what the AI has built.

On larger projects written by AI, trying to understand the system by prompting the AI again brings you back to the one-dimensional bandwidth of chat. The cost of going back to the drawing board is high. You can ask it to draw a diagram, but then you still have to talk to it through prompts.

Subtler problems get simpler with a 2D view: duplicated invariants causing bugs, a tangled mess of dependencies. They’re easy to untangle in 2D, and the AI knows better which code to use, change, or build on next time. And if you care about this kind of thing, it gives you peace of mind that you understand what just happened!

How it works

Your agent writes the spec. You read it as a picture, and answer on the picture.

  1. Agentsbtel open spec.sbtel

    Opens the diagram in your browser and prints a session id.

  2. YouIn the browser

    Comment on any block, port or level. Edit the diagram directly. Send it all as one review.

  3. Agentsbtel inbox $id --wait

    Reads your message, your comments and the spec you proposed.

  4. Agentsbtel open spec.sbtel --session $id

    Pushes the revision to the same tab and replies to each comment.

Then back to step 2, until the design is right. A review never writes your file; the agent does.

Generic blocks

A block is one part of the system. You can have them mean anything that you like to work with.

Code
features, modules, directories, files, functions, programs
Ideas
articles, prompts, documentation, personal notes
Processes
roles, tasks, agents

You just need to tell your agent what you mean.

A block can contain levels and other blocks, so you can progressively expand it with AI, from a subsystem down to a function. Its sketch is one plain sentence about what it is for. If the sentence needs an “and”, it is two blocks: one strand per block.

:type says what the block means.

The text your agent writes 
(block Notes :type "App"
  (~ Write notes and keep them. ~)
  (level :name "App"
    (block Editor)
    (block Preview))
  (level :name "Stored and formatted notes"
    (block Store :type "Module"
      (~ Keeps notes on disk. ~)
      (level :name "Reading and writing"
        (block save :type "Function")
        (block load :type "Function")))
    (block Formatter)))

Levels of abstraction

Low-level details stay in the lower levels.

Levels create an “abstraction barrier”: a dependency can only point downward, across levels. This is the stratified design of SICP. It gives you two things:

  1. An obvious path to mastering the system, top-down or bottom-up.
  2. Parts organized so they are easy to find when you need them.

One opinionated omission: there is no arrow from every block to each block it depends on. Levels give most of the benefit of those arrows without their noise, by defining which blocks may potentially depend on which ones.

Simple means not braided together (Rich Hickey, “Simple Made Easy”), so the blocks in a level stay independent of each other. When two must work as one, you declare them adjacent and the picture joins them with a line, as with Pricing and Tax here.

The text your agent writes 
(block Shop
  (level :name "Pages"
    (block Storefront))
  (level :name "Shopping"
    (block Cart) (block Checkout))
  (level :name "Prices"
    (adjacent (~ Tax is worked out on priced lines. ~)
      (block Pricing) (block Tax)))
  (level :name "Storage"
    (block Database)))

Messages

Messages let you slot in other blocks without depending on them explicitly.

For example, a block can take an abstract type, or a callback that a higher level implements: that is messaging. Messages can also stand for actual messages passing through a system. A message does not need a message channel as such; it is just a way to keep two implementations from knowing about each other.

A message type is data. A block takes messages in through ports on top of its card and sends them out from the bottom. Store sends a NoteSaved, Editor takes one, and something else connects them.

Point at a type to see everywhere that data travels. A NoteSaved carries a Note, drawn as a dot on its lower ear, so a note’s text goes wherever a NoteSaved goes. Where an assumption about some data lives, you can see all of it at once.

The text your agent writes 
(message Note (~ A note's title and text. ~))
(message NoteSaved (carries Note) (~ A note reached the disk. ~))
(level :name "App"
  (block Editor (port :in NoteSaved) (port :out Note))
  (block Preview (port :in Note)))
(level :name "Stored and formatted notes"
  (block Store (port :out NoteSaved))
  (block Formatter))
